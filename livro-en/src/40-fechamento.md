# Glossary

<dl class="gloss">
<dt>Bargue</dt><dd>Charles Bargue, author of the <i>Exercices au fusain</i>, a drawing course of sixty plates to be copied. Vincent copied all of it more than once between 1880 and 1881.</dd>
<dt>Black chalk</dt><dd>A natural black stone, in stick form. It gives a matte, warm black with no shine.</dd>
<dt>Broken contour</dt><dd>A contour line made in pieces, with small gaps, heavier on the underside of the form.</dd>
<dt>Carpenter's pencil</dt><dd>A flat pencil with a wide, rectangular lead. Used flat it makes a band; used on its edge it makes a thin line.</dd>
<dt>F and JH numbers</dt><dd>The catalogue numbers of Van Gogh's works. F comes from Jacob-Baart de la Faille's catalogue and JH from Jan Hulsker's. They identify a work without any confusion over titles.</dd>
<dt>Flow center</dt><dd>The point around which the flow lines turn: a star, an eye, the top of a hill.</dd>
<dt>Flow line</dt><dd>The direction the strokes follow in one zone of the drawing. The neighboring strokes stay nearly parallel to it.</dd>
<dt>Foxing</dt><dd>Brown spots that appear over time on old paper.</dd>
<dt>Graphite sketch</dt><dd>The light pencil drawing that came before the pen. On many Arles sheets it still shows beneath the ink.</dd>
<dt>Hatchings</dt><dd>In this book, the general name for the short, repeated marks of the pen: dot, dash, comma, flame, loop.</dd>
<dt>Iron gall ink</dt><dd>Ink made from iron salts and tannins, nearly black when fresh. Over time it fades to brown.</dd>
<dt>Laid paper</dt><dd>Paper made on a wire mold that leaves fine, closely set lines and widely spaced lines, visible against the light.</dd>
<dt>Lithographic crayon</dt><dd>A greasy crayon made for drawing on a lithographic stone. It gives the deepest black and does not erase. Vincent opened up the lights by scraping with a penknife.</dd>
<dt>Master study</dt><dd>A copy made to understand another artist's method.</dd>
<dt>Parallel hatching</dt><dd>Parallel lines that give value to an area. It becomes cross-hatching when a second layer comes in at another angle.</dd>
<dt>Perspective frame</dt><dd>A wooden frame strung with threads running vertically, horizontally and diagonally, fixed to stakes. Vincent used it from 1882 until at least 1888 (letters 253, 254 and 585).</dd>
<dt>Post-Impressionism</dt><dd>A name given later to the group of painters who started from Impressionism and went their own ways, among them Cézanne, Gauguin, Seurat and Van Gogh.</dd>
<dt>Quill</dt><dd>A goose feather cut for writing. It makes a finer, more flexible stroke than the reed. Vincent used it for details in the background.</dd>
<dt>Reed pen</dt><dd>A piece of dry cane cut at a slant, with a slit at the tip. It makes a broad, blunt, irregular stroke.</dd>
<dt>Reserved paper</dt><dd>An area left blank on purpose, like the sky in La Crau.</dd>
<dt>Scale ratio</dt><dd>How many times larger the biggest mark on the sheet is than the smallest. Below three, the space looks flat. Above five, the ground sinks away. In the La Crau drawings it goes beyond eight.</dd>
<dt>Translation</dt><dd>Redrawing a painting in another medium, choosing one mark for each color. Vincent did this with his own canvases and with works by Millet.</dd>
<dt>Wash</dt><dd>Ink diluted in water and applied with a brush, in patches. Van Gogh used it in The Hague. His mature drawings have almost none.</dd>
<dt>Willow charcoal</dt><dd>Charred sticks of willow or vine. They make broad masses and erase easily.</dd>
</dl>

# Sources and Further Reading

The main source on Van Gogh is Van Gogh himself. The complete edition of the letters, with English translation, notes and images of the sketches he drew in the margins, is at vangoghletters.org. The letter numbers cited in this book follow that edition.

The drawings can be seen in high resolution in the online collections of the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam, the Kröller-Müller Museum in Otterlo and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. Many of them are in the public domain on Wikimedia Commons.

To go further, three books:

<p class="ref">Colta Ives, Susan Alyson Stein, Sjraar van Heugten and Marije Vellekoop. <i>Vincent van Gogh: The Drawings</i>. New York: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2005. The catalogue of the major exhibition of his drawings, with reproductions of almost every sheet mentioned here.</p>

<p class="ref">Gerald M. Ackerman. <i>Charles Bargue: Drawing Course</i>. Paris: ACR Édition, 2003. Reproduces the plates Vincent copied.</p>

<p class="ref">Leo Jansen, Hans Luijten and Nienke Bakker (eds.). <i>Vincent van Gogh: The Letters</i>. London: Thames & Hudson, 2009. The printed edition of the letters, in six volumes.</p>

The catalogue data for the works (F and JH numbers, dimensions and collections) come from Wikidata and Wikimedia Commons.
