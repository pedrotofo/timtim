# 1. Materials

<aside class="vida"><h4>In His Life</h4><p>The Hague, 1882. Vincent tries everything he can find: carpenter's pencil, lithographic crayon, printer's ink. He writes to Theo that sometimes he needs to scratch down what he feels with a hard carpenter's pencil instead of a soft brush (letter 210).</p></aside>

Van Gogh drew with whatever was at hand: a carpenter's pencil in The Hague, black chalk in Nuenen, pens he cut himself from reeds in Arles. You don't need any of that. What makes a drawing look like his is the mark, and the mark comes out very well with a soft pencil and a fineliner, both sold in any art supply or office supply store.

<div class="kit"><h4>Getting Started</h4>
<ul>
<li><b>A 6B graphite pencil.</b> For sketches and for the figure exercises, in place of the carpenter's pencil, the chalk and the charcoal.</li>
<li><b>Three disposable fineliner pens: 0.1, 0.5 and 0.8.</b> The fine one for the background and details, the medium one for most of the drawing, the thick one for the foreground and heavy contours.</li>
<li><b>Letter or A4 printer paper</b> for practicing marks, and a pad of drawing paper, 140 to 180 g/m², in letter or A4 or in tabloid or A3, for the larger exercises.</li>
<li><b>A soft eraser</b> to clean up the sketch once the ink is dry.</li>
</ul></div>

In the exercises, materials always appear like this: "Pencil 6B" or "Fineliner 0.5". When an exercise calls for two or three widths, switch pens as the drawing moves from the background to the front.

## The 6B Pencil

A very soft pencil does almost everything Van Gogh's pencils and chalks did. Held flat, it covers large areas. Held upright, it makes a firm line. To imitate the carpenter's pencil line that swells and thins, sand the tip flat: the flat side gives a band, the edge gives a thin line, and a turn of the wrist takes you from one to the other in a single pass. That is where the changes in weight come from in the contours of *Sorrow* and *Worn Out*.

Value comes from density, not from pressure. Keep the same pressure and bring the strokes closer together to make a passage darker.

## The Fineliner

It stands in for the reed pen, the tool of the Arles and Saint-Rémy drawings. Vincent cut a piece of dry cane at a slant, "the same way you cut a goose quill" (letter 600), and used a quill for the fine lines in the distance (letter 618).

<figure class="mat"><img src="img/g1.jpg" alt="Cut reed pens, a pocketknife and shavings"><figcaption>Reed pens like the ones he used, cut with a pocketknife.</figcaption></figure>

The fineliner has two advantages: the width of the line stays the same, as with the reed, and the ink is black and solid, with no gradation. Each touch either leaves a mark or leaves nothing. The three widths play the part of his pens: the 0.1 is the quill for the background, the 0.5 is the medium reed, the 0.8 is the thick reed of the foreground.

The reed did two things on its own that you have to do on purpose. His ink ran out in the middle of a stroke, which started full and ended broken: with the fineliner, make short strokes and lift your hand quickly at the end. And there was no eraser, so treat every touch as final.

<figure class="mat"><img src="img/g3.jpg" alt="Fresh iron gall ink next to the same ink faded"><figcaption>The iron gall ink he used was nearly black. The brown we see in museums today is the work of time.</figcaption></figure>

## The Paper

For practicing marks, ordinary printer paper is fine. For the larger exercises, use drawing paper between 140 and 180 g/m², which takes ink without bleeding. If you can, work at tabloid or A3 size: many of the Arles drawings are close to 50 by 60 centimeters, and the broad gesture is part of the result.

## The Perspective Frame

In August 1882 Vincent had a wooden frame made with strings stretched across it, mounted on stakes he could drive into the sand of the dunes (letters 253 and 254). Seen through it, the subject already appears divided, and all he had to do was repeat the divisions on the paper.

<figure class="mat"><img src="img/g5.jpg" alt="Perspective frame set up in the dunes"><figcaption>A perspective frame mounted on stakes.</figcaption></figure>

To make your own, cut an 8 × 12 in (20 × 30 cm) window in a piece of cardboard and tape four black threads across it: one vertical and one horizontal through the center, and two diagonals from corner to corner. Always hold it at the same distance from your eye.

## On a Tablet

In Procreate or a similar app, pick a technical pen brush with a fixed size and no pressure variation. Set up three sizes, like the three pens: on a tabloid or A3 canvas at 300 dpi, about 8, 20 and 35 pixels. Draw on a light paper color instead of pure white, and don't use undo for an entire exercise.

# 2. Hatchings: An Alphabet of Marks

<aside class="vida"><h4>In His Life</h4><p>Arles, May to July 1888. Vincent climbs the hill of Montmajour again and again to draw the plain of La Crau. In July he sends Theo a roll with five large pen drawings from this series (letter 639).</p></aside>

In the Arles drawings, every thing has its own mark. The sky, the wheat, the sand, the roof and the beard never get the same kind of stroke. The sheet works like a map of textures with a key. I call these short, repeated marks hatchings.

The idea comes from the Japanese prints he collected in Paris. In them, rain is a scratch, water is a repeated wave and grass is a dot. In Arles, Vincent did the same with the reed pen, and you will do it with the fineliner.

## The Twelve Marks

There are twelve marks, and almost all of his drawings use some of them. Each has a typical place and a way of being made.

## Mark Size Is Distance

In the La Crau drawings, the foreground marks are about a centimeter long. At the horizon they are pinpricks. In between, each band of field has its own mark, a little smaller than the one in the band before. That shrinking is what makes the ground recede. There is almost no gradation of tone.

You can measure it. Divide the length of the largest mark on the sheet by the length of the smallest. In La Crau the ratio is more than eight. In *Garden with Flowers*, which shows a short distance, it is close to four. Below three, the space dies and the landscape turns into wallpaper.

<figure class="wide"><img src="img/fig-escala.jpg" alt="The same plain with marks of equal size, and with marks four and eight times smaller at the horizon"><figcaption>The same plain with a scale ratio of 1, 4 and 8 between the nearest mark and the farthest.</figcaption></figure>

## Value Comes from Density

In the mature drawings he did not blend. Dark comes from more marks per centimeter, all made with the same pressure. Light is paper left alone. This rule changes how you think about shadow: instead of pressing harder, you bring the strokes closer together.

## Reserved Paper

Almost every Arles sheet has a large, nearly empty area: the sky over La Crau, the road at Saintes-Maries, the whitewashed wall of the cottages. That empty space gives the eye a rest and makes the marked areas vibrate. Choose that area before you start and leave it blank.

<h2 class="newpage">The Six Rules of the Arles Sheet</h2>

Anyone who studies a lot of his drawings arrives at much the same working order for almost all of them.

<ol class="laws">
<li><b>A light graphite sketch.</b> Only the large divisions, no detail.</li>
<li><b>One mark for each surface.</b> Decide before you pick up the pen. It helps to write the key in a corner of the sheet.</li>
<li><b>From the horizon forward.</b> Start with the finest pen and move to the thicker ones as you come down into the foreground.</li>
<li><b>One pressure per zone.</b> Dark comes from bringing the marks closer, never from pressing harder.</li>
<li><b>One large blank area.</b> Choose which one it will be and leave it blank.</li>
<li><b>Blacks last.</b> Half a dozen accents in the foreground and in the hedges. Then stop.</li>
</ol>

# 3. Direction and Flow

<aside class="vida"><h4>In His Life</h4><p>Saint-Rémy, June 1889. Vincent writes that he can't get the cypresses out of his head and that he would like to do something with them like the sunflowers, because nobody has yet painted them the way he sees them (letter 783). That month he draws the cypresses and makes a pen copy of his Starry Night.</p></aside>

If you try to reproduce a Van Gogh drawing and feel that something is off, the problem is almost always the subject of this chapter. His marks follow a current, like iron filings around a magnet. Neighboring strokes run nearly parallel and don't cross. The direction changes slowly from one stroke to the next, and it describes the form without needing a contour.

In Saint-Rémy the short marks of Arles grew longer and began to link up with one another. Ground, tree and sky all follow the same movement.

## The Five Laws of Flow

<ol class="laws">
<li><b>Neighbors are parallel.</b> Two strokes side by side have almost the same angle, and changes of direction happen gradually.</li>
<li><b>Every flow has centers.</b> A star, an eye, the top of a hill. Near a center the strokes turn around it. Decide where the centers are before you draw.</li>
<li><b>Flow goes around bodies.</b> When it meets a cypress, a head or the moon, the lines open, pass along the sides and close again behind it, like water around a stone.</li>
<li><b>The mark gets shorter where the curve tightens.</b> Near a center the stroke is short. Where the current runs straight, it gets longer.</li>
<li><b>Movement needs a straight line.</b> In <i>The Starry Night</i>, the village is made of short, straight vertical dashes, and the sky seems to turn against it.</li>
</ol>

<figure class="flow"><img src="img/flow-starry.jpg" alt="The Starry Night with the lines of current drawn over it"><figcaption><b>The currents of <i>The Starry Night</i>.</b> The circles mark the centers: the moon and the great swirl. The arrows follow his dashes. Notice that no current crosses another, and that the vertical of the cypress cuts through the sky.</figcaption></figure>

## Why It Looks Wrong

Three mistakes show up in almost every attempt to imitate these drawings. The first is scattering strokes in random directions: the drawing gets Van Gogh's texture and none of his volume. The second is letting the dashes cross; even when the overall current is right, the sheet turns into straw. The third is making every mark the same size, and the landscape loses its depth.

## The Cypress

The cypress is a very good exercise in flow. It has no contour. The silhouette comes from hundreds of flame-shaped tufts added together, each made of two to four parallel strokes that rise and curl to one side. In the core of the tree the tufts press together until they almost merge. At the edges they loosen and let the paper show. The ground below repeats the movement in long waves.

## The Starry Night, Drawn

In June 1889, Vincent made a pen copy of the painting he had just finished. The drawing shows the flow without the color. The dashes in the sky follow currents around the stars and moon, each star has its own ring, the village stays still in straight strokes, and the cypress is the only dense mass on the sheet, linking the ground to the sky. The drawing was in the Kunsthalle Bremen until the Second World War and is now in the Shchusev State Museum of Architecture in Moscow.

## How to Study Flow in a Work

In front of any reproduction of his work, do three things before you copy. Find the centers and mark each with a dot. Over a printed copy, trace five or six very light lines of current that pass through his strokes. Then look for the straight element that provides the contrast. With that map ready, the copy comes out much more faithful than copying stroke by stroke.
