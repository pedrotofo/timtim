# Preface

Few artists have outlasted their own time the way Vincent van Gogh has. He sold very little while he was alive, died at 37 without seeing his name recognized, and today he is among the most viewed and most studied painters in the world. Maybe because of that reversal, his name catches our attention from the first time we hear it. The palette helps: no one mistakes it for anyone else's, with those deep blues that many people already call Van Gogh blue.

Art history places him among the Post-Impressionists, alongside Cézanne, Gauguin and Seurat. He arrived in Paris in 1886, absorbed what the Impressionists had done with light and color, and kept going, toward a kind of painting that shows what the artist feels in front of the subject. That is also why he is remembered as one of those who opened the way for Expressionism.

What interests me most about him, though, is on the sheets of paper. Van Gogh made more than a thousand drawings, and it was by drawing that he invented his language: dots, dashes, commas and swirls, each with a job to do and a direction that follows the form. He set aside a good part of the academic rules inherited from the Renaissance without giving up construction, and he arrived at a stroke you can recognize from a distance.

That makes him a generous teacher. Someone who is just starting out can enter his world through a single mark repeated with attention. Someone who already draws well will find in the same sheets a refinement that takes years to master. I wrote this book to cover that whole path, from the first scribbles to the master copies.

The book deals only with drawing, in pencil and in ink. That is why it is called *Van Gogh in Pencil*. His painting will have to wait for another occasion.

The aim is to learn to sketch like Van Gogh, to understand why each stroke is where it is, and to carry that language into the sketchbook or the tablet, in our everyday lives.

# How to Use This Book

The book has three parts. Part I tells, in a few pages, the story of the ten years Van Gogh spent drawing, with the focus on what changed in his stroke. Part II explains his language chapter by chapter: the materials, the marks, the direction of the stroke, the face, the self-portraits, the body, the order in which he built a sheet, and how to copy a master. There are 71 exercises in four levels. The Level 1 exercises are short and appear at the end of each chapter of Part II as *First Steps*: you read the theory and practice it right away. The exercises for Levels 2 to 4 are longer and are gathered in Part III, *Studies*, for when you have the language in hand.

## Where to Start

If you are just beginning to draw, read the book in order and do the *First Steps* in each chapter before moving on. They are short and train one mark at a time.

If you already draw, you can go straight to Level 2, which brings together the 17 essential exercises of the method. Go back to the *First Steps* whenever a mark doesn't come out the way you want.

Levels 3 and 4 are for when the essentials are solid. Level 4 holds the master studies: long copies of his works and the final project, in which you take the language into your own world.

## The Model Drawings

Each exercise starts from a model drawing made in the style of his sheets, and some show the stages of construction, from the graphite sketch to the finish. Van Gogh's own works are identified by title, date and, where known, their catalogue numbers and collection.

## Drawing Over a Model

This book was made to be read, on an e-reader too. To draw over a model, print the exercise page and work on top of it with tracing paper, or open the page in an app such as Procreate and draw on a layer above it, with the model faded. For most exercises, though, it is best to draw beside the book, looking at the model.

## The Letters

Van Gogh wrote more than eight hundred letters, most of them to his brother Theo, and many of them talk about drawing. When this book quotes a letter, it gives the letter's number in the complete annotated edition maintained by the Van Gogh Museum and the Huygens Institute, available at vangoghletters.org. The quotations are the author's own free translations, made from that edition's English translation.
