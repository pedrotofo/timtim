# Ten Years of Drawing

Vincent Willem van Gogh was born on March 30, 1853, in Zundert, a village in the south of Holland, the son of a Protestant minister. He drew as a boy, as many children did at the time, but he only decided to become an artist at 27. Ten years passed between that decision, in 1880, and his death, in 1890. In that time he made about 850 paintings and more than a thousand drawings.

This chapter follows those ten years through what changed in his stroke. Each city he lived in left something in his drawing, and you will meet those things again in the chapters that follow.

## Before Art, 1869 to 1880

At 16 Vincent went to work for Goupil & Cie, an art dealing firm, at its branch in The Hague. He later moved through the London and Paris branches and spent six years among prints and reproductions. He was dismissed in 1876. He tried being a teacher in England, a clerk in a bookshop in Dordrecht and a theology student in Amsterdam, and ended up as a lay preacher among the coal miners of the Borinage, in Belgium.

There, with no work and no money, he began to draw the miners. In August 1880 he wrote to Theo asking for Charles Bargue's *Exercices au fusain*, a drawing course in plates to be copied (letter 156). Theo, four years younger and also employed by Goupil, began supporting him from then on and would support him until the end.

## Brussels and Etten, 1880 to 1881

In Brussels, Vincent studied anatomy and perspective and met the painter Anthon van Rappard, who became a friend and correspondent. He copied Millet, the French painter of peasants who would be his model for the rest of his life. In Etten, where his parents lived, he redid the entire series of sixty Bargue plates (letter 171) and drew the local laborers from life.

The drawings from these months are stiff and too correct. He knew it himself, and he kept copying.

## The Hague, 1881 to 1883

At the end of 1881 he moved to The Hague and took a few lessons from Anton Mauve, an important painter of the Hague School and a relative of the family by marriage. The relationship did not last long. Vincent went to live with Sien Hoornik, a pregnant woman with a daughter, who posed for many drawings, among them *Sorrow*.

The Hague was his school of black and white. He collected prints from English magazines such as *The Graphic*, tried out the carpenter's pencil and the lithographic crayon, and fixed graphite with milk to take away its shine (letters 217 and 297). He had the perspective frame made that he carried out to the dunes (letters 253 and 254), and he drew the old men from the almshouse, whom he called orphan men. In a letter of October 1882 he described the effort of drawing as working your way through an invisible iron wall (letter 274).

## Drenthe and Nuenen, 1883 to 1885

In September 1883 he left Sien and went to Drenthe, a region of peat bogs in the north of the country. He stayed three months and went back to his parents' house, by then in Nuenen. There he drew and painted weavers and peasants for two years, leading up to *The Potato Eaters* of 1885.

In Nuenen he changed the way he built a figure. He read that, according to Delacroix, the ancients started a form from the centers, from the masses, and not from the line (letter 494), and he began drawing from the inside out. He also wrote that he would be in despair if his figures were academic and correct (letter 515).

## Antwerp and Paris, 1885 to 1888

After three months in Antwerp, where he attended the academy for a short time, Vincent arrived in Paris in March 1886 and moved in with Theo. He went to Fernand Cormon's studio, met Émile Bernard, Toulouse-Lautrec and Paul Signac, and saw the Impressionists and Neo-Impressionists up close. The dark palette of Holland lightened within a few months.

What weighed most on his drawing was Japan. Vincent and Theo collected hundreds of cheap Japanese prints, and he copied two Hiroshige prints in oil. In those prints, each surface has its own graphic pattern. That idea would become the basis of the alphabet of marks he used in Arles.

## Arles, 1888 to 1889

In February 1888 Vincent went to Arles, in the south of France, looking for light and a cheaper life. He began drawing with pens he cut from reeds, "the same way you cut a goose quill" (letter 600). He used very thick reed pens and a quill for the fine lines in the background (letter 618). He climbed the hill of Montmajour to draw the plain of La Crau and spent a few days in Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer, where he made the drawing of the boats on the beach in one hour (letter 620). In the same letter he remarked that the Japanese draw fast, like lightning.

In the summer he redrew many of his own paintings in pen to send to Theo and to friends. In October, Paul Gauguin came to live with him in the Yellow House. Their time together ended in December, with the crisis in which Vincent cut off part of his left ear. He was hospitalized more than once in the months that followed.

## Saint-Rémy, 1889 to 1890

In May 1889 Vincent voluntarily committed himself to the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole, in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence. He drew the garden, the fields behind the wall, the olive trees and the cypresses, which he said were as beautiful in line and proportion as an Egyptian obelisk (letter 783). The short stroke of Arles stretched into curves that tie together earth, tree and sky.

In the months when he could not go outside, he copied prints after Millet and Delacroix, which he compared to a musician interpreting another composer's score (letter 805). Painting from Millet's drawings was, for him, translating them into another language (letter 816).

## Auvers-sur-Oise, 1890

In May 1890 he moved to Auvers-sur-Oise, near Paris, under the care of the doctor Paul Gachet. In a little over two months he made about seventy paintings and many drawings, and he asked Theo to send him the Bargue plates again (letter 874). On July 27 he was wounded by a gunshot to the chest, and he died two days later, on July 29, 1890.

Theo died six months later. His widow, Johanna van Gogh-Bonger, kept the works, organized exhibitions and published the letters in 1914. It was through her that the drawings and the letters reached the public.
