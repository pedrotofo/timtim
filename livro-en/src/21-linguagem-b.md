# 4. The Face as Terrain

<aside class="vida"><h4>In His Life</h4><p>Arles, August 1888. Joseph Roulin, a postal worker at the Arles railway station, becomes a friend and a model. Vincent paints him at the end of July and then redraws the portrait in pen. That same summer he draws the peasant Patience Escalier, the girl he calls La Mousmé and a Zouave soldier.</p></aside>

In the pen portraits from Arles, Vincent used the same alphabet on the face that he used on the landscape. He treats the face as terrain: it has planes, and the strokes run over it like contour lines on a map. Each part has its own current, and the volume comes from the change of direction between one part and the next.

## The Direction Map

<figure class="flow"><img src="img/flow-roulin.jpg" alt="Portrait of Joseph Roulin with the directions of the strokes drawn over it"><figcaption><b>The direction map on Roulin.</b> The eyes are centers, with rings around them. The nose runs straight down. Mustache and beard open downward and outward like a fan. The cap follows the curve of the head, the coat runs down with the lapel and the background is an even lattice.</figcaption></figure>

The eyes are the centers of two swirls. The dashes turn in rings around the eye socket and spread across the cheekbone. The upper eyelid is the thickest line on the face, and the iris is the darkest spot on the whole sheet.

On the forehead, the strokes make wide, nearly horizontal arcs that drop toward the temples. At the hairline the direction changes all at once and the hair rises like a flame. The border between skin and hair shows only through that change, with no contour.

On the nose, the strokes run straight down the bridge. On the side they turn and follow the swirl of the eye. Around the tip they make an arc. The nostril is a short, heavy contour.

The cheeks carry on the rings of the eyes and move down toward the chin. On the shadow side the strokes sit closer together, always with the same pressure.

The mustache radiates out and down from under the nose. The beard fans out from the mouth in commas that run away from it. The line between the lips is a heavy contour.

The coat is usually the darkest and coarsest zone, in cross-hatching that follows the lapel. The background is always more even and lighter than the figure: lattice, dots, rays or a swirl.

<figure><img src="img/roulin.jpg" alt="Portrait of Joseph Roulin, pen drawing"><figcaption><i>Portrait of Joseph Roulin</i>, Arles, August 1888. Reed pen and quill, ink and black chalk. J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles. F1458, JH1536.</figcaption></figure>

## The Four Substances

The Roulin portrait brings together almost everything this chapter describes. There are four substances and four marks: the beard in curves that fan downward, the coat in coarse cross-hatching, the skin in dots and dashes and the background in a light lattice. Beard and coat have nearly the same value, yet they never blur together, because the mark changes. Cover the face with your hand and you can still tell what each part is made of.

The cap is almost all contour, a calm zone among so much texture. The eyes, with their heavy contour, hold the sheet together.

<figure><img src="img/escalier2.jpg" alt="Portrait of Patience Escalier, pen drawing"><figcaption><i>Portrait of Patience Escalier</i>, Arles, August 1888. The hat is left almost blank and the whole background is dotted, which pushes the head forward. Harvard Art Museums. F1460, JH1549.</figcaption></figure>

## Few Marks

Not every portrait is dense. In *La Mousmé*, the girl's face is almost blank paper, with heavy contour only on the eyes, eyebrows, nostrils and mouth and a few dashes under the cheekbone. The blouse is striped and the skirt dotted. On a young face, fewer marks get a better result.

<figure><img src="img/mousme.jpg" alt="La Mousmé, pen drawing"><figcaption><i>La Mousmé</i>, Arles, 1888. Pen and ink. F1503.</figcaption></figure>

## Light from the Side

With strong light from one side, the lit side gets half the marks, or fewer. The shadow side gets all of them, closer together, at the same pressure. The direction of the strokes keeps following the planes on both sides. That is how the face gains volume without any smudged area.

<div class="obra" markdown="1">

## Where to Start a Face

<ol class="laws">
<li><b>The eyes and the contour of the hat or hair.</b> These are the darkest and sharpest marks, and they pin down the likeness.</li>
<li><b>Beard or hair.</b> In the direction of the map: the hair rises, the beard fans downward.</li>
<li><b>The coat.</b> The coarsest zone on the sheet, in hatching that follows the lapel.</li>
<li><b>The skin.</b> It comes near the end and gets fewer marks than you expect.</li>
<li><b>The background.</b> Last, and always lighter than the coat.</li>
</ol>

Chapter 7 shows this order in a complete portrait.

<figure><img src="img/roulin2.jpg" alt="Joseph Roulin seated, pen drawing"><figcaption><i>Portrait of Joseph Roulin</i> (seated), Arles, August 1888. Pen and ink. Los Angeles County Museum of Art. The same hierarchy in a full figure: firm face and beard, dark coat, almost empty background.</figcaption></figure>

</div>

# 5. The Self-Portraits

<aside class="vida"><h4>In His Life</h4><p>Paris, 1886 to 1888. With no money to pay models, Vincent paints himself dozens of times in front of the mirror. It is also in Paris that his brushstroke breaks up into separate dashes, under the influence of the Neo-Impressionists.</p></aside>

Van Gogh made more than 35 self-portraits, nearly all of them between 1886 and 1889. They are paintings, so this chapter is an exception in a drawing book. The reason is practical: in the paintings from this period each brushstroke is a visible dash, with a direction and a size. They show the flow across the face more clearly than the few drawn self-portraits do, and they translate directly into pencil or pen.

Three solutions are worth following, in order.

<div class="obra" markdown="1">

## 1887: The Halo

In *Self-Portrait with Grey Felt Hat*, painted in Paris in the winter of 1887 to 1888, the dashes are short and separate. On the face, they turn around the eyes and run down the beard. In the background, they form rings around the head, a halo of marks that sets it apart. The brim of the hat has dashes that go all the way around, and the crown has arcs that follow the dome.

<figure><img src="img/sp_felt.jpg" alt="Self-Portrait with Grey Felt Hat"><figcaption><i>Self-Portrait with Grey Felt Hat</i>, Paris, 1887 to 1888. Oil on canvas. Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam. F344, JH1353.</figcaption></figure>

</div>

<div class="obra" markdown="1">

In the Chicago self-portrait, from the spring of 1887, the solution is different: the dashes on the face radiate from the nose like rays, and the background is dotted, with dots larger than the marks on the face.

<figure><img src="img/sp_chi.jpg" alt="Self-portrait of 1887, Art Institute of Chicago"><figcaption><i>Self-Portrait</i>, Paris, spring 1887. Oil on wood. Art Institute of Chicago. F345, JH1249.</figcaption></figure>

</div>

<div class="obra" markdown="1">

## 1888: The Calm Background

In Arles, in the self-portrait dedicated to Paul Gauguin, the background is almost flat. The face, with the head shaved, takes on firm planes and a heavy contour.

<figure><img src="img/sp_gauguin.jpg" alt="Self-Portrait Dedicated to Paul Gauguin"><figcaption><i>Self-Portrait Dedicated to Paul Gauguin</i>, Arles, September 1888. Oil on canvas. Harvard Art Museums. F476, JH1581.</figcaption></figure>

</div>

<div class="obra" markdown="1">

## 1889: The Swirl

In the self-portrait of September 1889, painted in Saint-Rémy and now in the Musée d'Orsay, the background turns into moving water. The coat joins the same current, in wavy lines just like those of the background, and figure and background nearly merge. Only the face stays still, with shorter, firmer marks. In his work this is the clearest case of the fifth law of flow: movement needs something still in order to show, and here the still element is the face itself.

<figure><img src="img/sp_orsay.jpg" alt="Self-portrait of 1889, Musée d'Orsay"><figcaption><i>Self-Portrait</i>, Saint-Rémy, September 1889. Oil on canvas. Musée d'Orsay, Paris. F627, JH1772.</figcaption></figure>

</div>

<h2 class="newpage">What Holds for All of Them</h2>

The eyes are the darkest and sharpest point. Around them, everything else can move. Hair and beard are usually the same mark, the flame, in opposite directions: the hair rises and moves away from the face, the beard drops and moves away from the mouth.

He also painted himself sometimes turned to the left, sometimes to the right. Do the same, because your hand finds the opposite side awkward.

# 6. The Body in Rings

<aside class="vida"><h4>In His Life</h4><p>Nuenen, summer 1885. Vincent draws peasants digging, reaping and gleaning, in black chalk on large sheets. He writes to Theo that he would despair if his figures were academic and correct (letter 515).</p></aside>

Van Gogh's figure work comes down to two operations. The first is seeing the body as oval masses: torso, hips, thighs, arms, each one becomes a barrel or an egg. In Nuenen he read that the ancients began a form from the centers, from the masses, and not from the line (letter 494), and he started drawing that way. The second operation is hatching in rings: the strokes wrap around each mass, perpendicular to its axis, like the hoops on a barrel. On the shadow side the rings sit closer together.

The heavy contour goes in last and on one side only, where the form turns or rests. On the leg in *Worn Out* the hatching curves like the elastic of a sock, and the leg looks round even though the tone is nearly the same from end to end.

<figure><img src="img/wornout.jpg" alt="Worn Out, pencil drawing"><figcaption><i>Worn Out</i>, The Hague, November 1882. Pencil on watercolor paper. Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam. F997.</figcaption></figure>

<figure><img src="img/peasant.jpg" alt="Peasant woman bending, black chalk"><figcaption><i>Peasant Woman Bending</i>, Nuenen, 1885. Black chalk. Museum Folkwang, Essen. The back and the hips are two oval masses, and the hatching changes direction with each one.</figcaption></figure>

## The Weight Below

His figures have big hands, heavy feet and enormous wooden clogs. The visual weight sits low and plants the figure on the ground. In The Hague and in Nuenen the ground is a handful of scratches, and the background stays empty.

## Emotion in the Pose

In *Sorrow* and in *Worn Out* the face is hidden. All the emotion is in the curve of the back. Before any detail, find the line that sums up the pose and exaggerate it a little.

<figure><img src="img/sorrow.jpg" alt="Sorrow"><figcaption><i>Sorrow</i>, The Hague, April 1882. Pencil, pen and ink. The New Art Gallery Walsall. A single line, continuous and angular, thicker underneath the forms.</figcaption></figure>

# 7. How He Built a Sheet

<aside class="vida"><h4>In His Life</h4><p>Arles, June 1888. Vincent comes back from Saintes-Maries with the drawing of the boats and writes that in Paris he would never have done it in an hour (letter 620). The speed comes from a working order he repeated on almost every sheet.</p></aside>

The pen drawings from Arles did not start straight in ink. First came a light graphite sketch, which still shows underneath on many sheets. Then the ink went in at the background and moved forward, and the strongest blacks were left for the end. The sequences below show that order in three model drawings, in four stages each.

# 8. Master Copy

<aside class="vida"><h4>In His Life</h4><p>Saint-Rémy, September 1889. Unable to leave the asylum, Vincent copies prints after Millet and Delacroix. He compares the work to that of a musician who plays Beethoven and adds his own personal interpretation (letter 805).</p></aside>

Van Gogh learned to draw by copying. In his first years he redid the whole series of Bargue plates (letter 171) and copied Millet many times. In Saint-Rémy, by then a mature artist, he went back to Millet and painted from his drawings, which he called translating them into another language (letter 816). For him, copying meant understanding why each mark is where it is.

The master studies in this book, in Level 4, each call for one of these five methods. Follow the one the exercise names, or use them in order, from the most guided to the freest.

## 1. With a Grid

Divide the reproduction into squares of 1¼ to 1½ in (3 to 4 cm). Repeat the grid on your sheet, in the same proportion, in very light graphite. Copy square by square only the big divisions: horizon, contours, axes. Erase the grid before you start the marks.

<figure><img src="img/g7.jpg" alt="Reproduction with a grid next to a sheet with the same grid"><figcaption>A grid over the reproduction and the same grid on the sheet.</figcaption></figure>

## 2. By Sight-Measuring

Pick a unit, such as the height of the head, and measure everything against it with the pencil held at arm's length. Use the pencil to copy angles too: tilt it until it lines up with the line and carry the angle over to the paper. It is slower than the grid and teaches more. Use it for copies of faces.

<figure><img src="img/g8.jpg" alt="Hand measuring with a pencil"><figcaption>The pencil at arm's length works as a ruler and a protractor.</figcaption></figure>

## 3. In Stages

Follow the building order from Chapter 7: light sketch, background in ink, middle, front and accents. Stop at the end of each stage and compare.

## 4. By Substance

Before you start, write the drawing's key in the corner of the sheet: sky, wheat, skin, beard, coat. Then copy one whole substance at a time, across the entire sheet, before moving to the next. That way the mark in each zone stays consistent from start to finish.

## 5. From Memory

Look at the model for five minutes and describe to yourself the order of the zones and the direction of each one. Hide the model and draw for fifteen minutes. Compare and note what you forgot. Repeat the next day with the same model. In 1890, unable to go out, Vincent filled sheets with peasants and northern landscapes drawn from memory. Memory throws out the detail and keeps the rhythm, and rhythm is what these drawings ask for.

## Translating Painting into Drawing

In the summer of 1888 he redrew many of his own paintings in pen to send to Theo and his friends, and for each color on the canvas he had to choose a mark. Choose one of his paintings, make a table matching each color to a mark and redraw the whole thing in ink. Exercise R19 and the self-portraits in Level 4 use this method.

<figure><img src="img/harvest.jpg" alt="The Harvest, drawing for Émile Bernard"><figcaption><i>The Harvest</i>, drawing for Émile Bernard, Arles, 1888. The yellow of the wheat became dashes, the green became commas and the fence became a comb.</figcaption></figure>
